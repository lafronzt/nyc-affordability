## Two different questions hiding behind one phrase

"How much salary do I need to live in NYC" is really two separate questions:

1. **What income does a landlord (or co-op board, or lender) require to say yes?** This is a *screening* number — a gross-income multiple set to protect the landlord, not a budget built around your actual spending.
2. **What income does it actually take to cover my expenses, hit my savings goal, and have money left over after tax?** This is a *take-home* number — it depends on your real budget and NYC's tax stack, not a landlord's formula.

These two numbers are rarely close to each other, and the gap is the whole point of this guide.

## Question 1: What a landlord's income rule requires

Most NYC landlords use some version of the [40x rent rule](/guides/nyc-40x-rent-rule/): your annual gross income must be at least 40 times the monthly rent. For a $3,000/month apartment, that's a $120,000/year income requirement — full stop, regardless of your other expenses, debts, or savings goals. It's a screening threshold, not a budget.

## Question 2: What it actually takes to cover your budget

Say your real numbers for that same $3,000/month apartment look like this: $1,200/month for everything else (food, transit, subscriptions, the rest of life) and a $300/month savings goal. That's $4,500/month, or $54,000/year, that actually needs to land in your bank account *after* tax.

Working backwards through federal, NY State, NYC resident tax, and FICA for a single filer with no 401(k) or HSA, hitting that $54,000/year take-home target takes a gross salary of about **$72,375/year** ($6,031/month) — using this site's own [Required Salary Calculator](/required-salary/) math.

## The two numbers, side by side

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Income to qualify for the apartment (40x rule) | $120,000/year |
| Income to actually cover that budget after tax | ~$72,375/year |
| What $120,000/year actually nets after tax | ~$82,882/year ($6,907/month) |

The landlord's number is nearly **$50,000/year higher** than what it actually takes to cover the stated budget — because the 40x rule isn't trying to answer "can this person cover $4,500/month," it's building in a large cushion against risk, rent increases, and every other draw on income a landlord can't see. If you qualify at 40x, you're very likely to also comfortably clear your actual budget; the reverse isn't guaranteed, which is why it's worth checking both numbers, not just the one a landlord asks for.

## Why NYC's number is higher than almost anywhere else

The gap between gross salary and take-home pay is bigger in NYC than in most of the country because NYC residents stack four separate withholdings on every paycheck: [federal income tax](/salary/), NY State income tax, [NYC's own local resident tax](/glossary/nyc-local-income-tax/) (3.078%–3.876%, on top of state tax — money a commuter from New Jersey or Westchester never owes), and [FICA](/glossary/fica-tax/) (Social Security + Medicare). By the time all four come out, [take-home pay](/glossary/take-home-pay/) commonly lands around 65-75% of gross — which is exactly why a "salary needed to live in NYC" answer has to start from a real take-home target, not a rule of thumb about gross income.
