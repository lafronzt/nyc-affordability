## What rent stabilization actually does

A rent-stabilized apartment isn't rent-controlled (a much smaller, older category with its own separate rules), and it isn't the same as "affordable housing" with an income cap. Stabilization is a regulatory status attached to the apartment itself, generally covering units in buildings with six or more units built before 1974, plus some newer buildings that received tax incentives in exchange for stabilizing their units. It changes two things a market-rate lease doesn't have: a legal cap on how much your rent can increase at renewal, and a right to renew your lease at all; a stabilized landlord generally can't simply decline to renew or push you out to reset the rent to market rate.

## How much your rent can actually go up

The NYC Rent Guidelines Board (RGB) votes once a year on the maximum percentage increase landlords can apply at lease renewal, and that vote applies uniformly to every stabilized unit citywide. An individual landlord can't set a higher number no matter what the local market is doing.

On June 25, 2026, the board voted 7-1 to adopt **Order #58, a full rent freeze**: for leases commencing October 1, 2026 through September 30, 2027, the maximum increase is **0% on both one-year and two-year renewals**. It's the first time the board has frozen both lease terms at once. Past freezes (in 2015, 2016, and 2020) applied to one-year leases only. The previous order still governs leases that commenced before October 1, 2026:

| Lease commencing | 1-year lease | 2-year lease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 1, 2026 – Sep 30, 2027 (Order #58) | 0% | 0% |
| Oct 1, 2025 – Sep 30, 2026 (Order #57) | 3% | 4.5% |

What matters is the date your renewal lease *commences*, not when you sign it. A renewal starting September 1, 2026 still falls under Order #57's caps. These are ceilings, not automatic increases. A landlord can renew at the same rent or lower, but legally cannot exceed the board's cap for that renewal period. The RGB revisits these numbers every year, so check the [Rent Guidelines Board's current order](https://rentguidelinesboard.cityofnewyork.us/rent-guidelines/) before assuming the freeze carries into your next cycle. One more caveat: a group of landlords has sued to overturn the freeze. As of October 1, 2026 it remains in effect, the judge declined to restore the old 3% and 4.5% caps in the meantime, and a ruling is expected before the end of the year. If the freeze were overturned, the board would have to vote again, and what that would mean for renewals already signed isn't settled.

## How to check if your apartment is actually stabilized

Building age and unit count are a starting point, not proof. The most reliable way to check is to request your apartment's rent history directly from the NYS Division of Housing and Community Renewal (DHCR), which tracks registered rent-stabilized units and their filed rent amounts going back years. A current lease that doesn't mention stabilization, or a landlord who tells you it isn't stabilized, isn't authoritative on its own; plenty of disputes over stabilization status get resolved only after a DHCR rent history request.

## Succession and other protections

Rent stabilization also carries rights beyond the renewal cap: a stabilized tenant generally has a right to a lease renewal (a landlord needs a specific, legally recognized reason to decline one), a family member who's lived with the tenant can in some circumstances have succession rights to take over the lease, and any additional fees a landlord tries to charge beyond legally permitted amounts can be challenged as [key money](/glossary/key-money/) or an illegal overcharge. None of this applies automatically to a market-rate unit, which is one reason it's worth confirming your actual status rather than assuming based on the building.

## What this doesn't mean

Stabilization doesn't freeze your rent, doesn't cap it based on your income, and doesn't guarantee it's below market rate. Some long-vacant, heavily renovated stabilized units have legal rents that are close to or even above comparable market-rate apartments nearby, especially after years of compounding legal increases. It's a process protection and an increase cap, not an income-based subsidy. For the latter, see how [AMI-based affordable housing](/guides/nyc-ami-housing-connect-explained/) works instead.
